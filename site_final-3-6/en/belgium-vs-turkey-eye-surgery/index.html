Eye laser surgery: Belgium or Turkey?

Eye laser surgery costs three to five times less in Istanbul than in Belgium. This price gap draws thousands of European patients every year. This page compares the two destinations on the criteria that matter: medical framework, continuity of follow-up, actual total cost once travel is included, and management of complications.

The essentials in 30 seconds

  • Headline price gap: Femto-LASIK 700 to 1500 € per eye in Turkey vs 1700 to 2250 € per eye in Belgium, largely absorbed by travel and hidden costs.
  • Regulatory framework: EU directive and INAMI in Belgium, variable extraterritorial framework in Turkey.
  • Postoperative follow-up: accessible in Belgium, almost impossible from abroad.
  • Management of complications: treatments available on site in Belgium (cross-linking, partial corneal graft), retreatment included up to 6 months, costs covered by public and complementary health insurance. Outside the EU, surgical revision is borne by the patient.
  • Travel: Brussels 2 h from London by train, 1 h 15 by air from Zurich or Geneva.
Context

Why the headline price attracts

Istanbul has become an ophthalmic medical tourism destination for one main reason: cost. Lower labour costs, lower fixed overheads and the absence of regulatory constraints equivalent to those of the European Union make it possible to offer refractive surgery at a tariff three to five times lower than in Western Europe. The most visible centres run on a volume model, sometimes performing more than a hundred procedures per day.

Marketing plays a central role in the phenomenon. Targeted campaigns aimed at the United Kingdom, Germany, France and the Netherlands push an all-inclusive package (surgery, hotel, transfers, sometimes flight) at a price still below the cost of a single procedure at home. What is highlighted is the price. What is not, and what this page focuses on, is what that price does not cover.

What the all-inclusive package hides

A headline price. The advertised price often applies to the most basic version of the technique (standard LASIK, without femtosecond), with a surcharge for Femto-LASIK or for higher corrections.

An express exam. The preoperative exam is sometimes done on the morning of surgery, leaving little time to interpret the data carefully or step back if the profile is at risk.

Limited follow-up. The D+1 check is included, later controls have to be organised remotely or re-created locally.

A commercial decision. In some structures, a non-medical adviser presents the offer before the patient meets the surgeon.

Headline price and total cost

What the low price does not always cover

The price advertised by Turkish centres (often between 700 and 1500 € per eye for a Femto-LASIK) does not match the total project cost for a European patient. Return flights, three to five hotel nights depending on the format offered, meals, transfers and any days of leave used must be added. For a patient who wants a proper preoperative exam and then a return visit for the procedure itself, that means two trips rather than one.

The economic model of high-volume centres relies on standardisation. The initial consultation can last fifteen minutes. Operative parameters are often set from pre-defined protocols. The staff who present the offer and set up appointments are not always medically qualified. This model is efficient for simple, predictable profiles. It is less so for a patient whose cornea is atypical, whose dry eye is marked, or whose indication would benefit from a second look after a cooling-off period.

The most sensitive point remains relational continuity. The surgeon who operated is the one who should, in principle, be reachable in case of postoperative doubt. That continuity is possible in Belgium with a simple call or a follow-up consultation. It is far harder to establish from another country, where the involvement of an interpreter, the time difference and the physical distance change the nature of the relationship.

The Belgian medical framework

What is concretely regulated in Belgium

Before comparing differences, it is worth stating what the Belgian medical framework actually covers in practice. Each element listed below is an effective guarantee for the patient, not a statement of intent.

Safety in Belgium, point by point

Complete traceability of the file. Every preoperative measurement, every laser parameter used, every postoperative check is recorded in a single file, accessible for all the years that follow the procedure.

Mandatory professional liability insurance. Any surgeon practising in Belgium is covered by professional liability insurance. If medical negligence is recognised, the patient has a structured route to compensation.

Order of Physicians framework. The code of conduct applies to every act, with recourse and sanctions possible. Contradictory medical expertise is accessible within a reasonable timeframe.

INAMI accreditation and continuing education. Maintaining approval requires a set number of continuing-education hours each year, with verifiable records.

Full hospital plateau. Anaesthetists in the operating room, 24/7 emergency access, multidisciplinary team (retina, cornea, glaucoma) available within the Chirec network if needed.

Treatments for complications available on site. Cross-linking, lamellar corneal graft, laser retreatment, flap repositioning: each corrective procedure can be performed by the operating surgeon or a colleague nearby.

Fast second opinion. A consultation with another Belgian ophthalmologist can be arranged within 48 hours. Patients who want to verify an indication can do so without leaving the country.

Retreatment included up to 6 months. If the visual result needs an adjustment within six months after the procedure, the retreatment is covered by the initial fee.

Where the difference lies

What separates the two care pathways

The difference plays out in the pathway that surrounds the procedure: regulatory framework, continuity of follow-up, language of consultation, management of any complication.

Regulatory framework and legal recourse

Belgium applies the European medical devices directive, the INAMI rules and the Order of Physicians' code of conduct. A claim for medical negligence sits within an identified legal framework, with expert assessment possible and mandatory professional liability insurance. Turkey has its own framework, but it operates within a different legal system, with delays and language barriers that make recourse practically difficult for a foreign patient.

Continuity of follow-up

A complete refractive surgery pathway includes a preoperative exam, the procedure and a sequence of postoperative controls: D+1, D+7, D+30, M+3 and M+12. That is a minimum of five consultations, without counting any additional visits in case of complication. In Belgium these consultations are accessible in continuity of the record, with the surgeon who operated. From Turkey, the organisation requires either repeated trips or a handover to a local ophthalmologist who inherits a record they did not open.

Language and cultural setting

A consultation in French, Dutch or English, within a healthcare system the patient already knows, changes the quality of the exchange. Understanding the nuances of a clinical explanation, asking a sensitive question about a postoperative doubt, or reading an operative report in one's native language all carry weight in the medical relationship.

The true cost of a complication

Serious complications in refractive surgery remain rare (infectious keratitis, corneal ectasia, flap displacement, stromal opacity). When they do occur, treatment can require months of care, partial corneal grafting or cross-linking. In Belgium, this care is covered by the social security and the mutual health insurer. For a patient operated outside the EU, surgical revision and treatments are self-funded, unless specifically agreed with a travel insurer. The cumulative cost can easily exceed ten times the amount saved on the initial procedure.

What is at stake if a complication occurs

What a patient stands to lose if a complication occurs after Turkey

A serious complication remains rare in refractive surgery. When it does occur in a patient operated abroad, several losses add to the initial medical cost and shift the economic equation well beyond the numbers in the table.

Potentially permanent visual loss. Undetected corneal ectasia, a late infection or a mishandled flap dislocation can lead to a partial or full corneal graft. Even performed well, this reoperation rarely restores vision to the level of an uncomplicated eye.

Medical costs of revision borne by the patient. Surgical revision of a refractive act performed outside the European Union is not covered by INAMI or by Belgian complementary insurers. The patient pays for the new procedure, the associated treatments and the follow-up.

Legal recourse very difficult. A liability action against a Turkish centre requires a local procedure, in a foreign language, with delays measured in years. Most patients give up before completing the first procedural step.

Loss of the retreatment guarantee. Belgian fees include a retreatment up to six months after the procedure. This guarantee does not exist in the commercial model of high-volume centres, or it requires a physical return on site that multiplies the costs.

Professional impact. For a pilot, a soldier, a police officer, a surgeon or any profession with high visual demands, an unresolved complication can affect fitness for duty. Having to justify a failed surgery to an employer or a regulator adds a further difficulty.

Social costs. Months of sick leave, workstation adjustments, temporary or permanent visual aids: the economic consequences can exceed tens of thousands of euros, out of all proportion to the 1 500 € saved initially.

Incomplete operative file. The Belgian surgeon who takes over management of the complication does not always have access to the exact laser parameters used, the preoperative topographic data or the detailed operative report. Follow-up is resumed without the information that would have helped optimise the response.

Psychological impact. The sense of having made the wrong choice, guilt and difficulty in trusting a medical team again are frequent in patients faced with a complication after surgery performed abroad.

Where Belgium is the better call

When Belgium is the better choice

Several profiles clearly benefit from being managed on site. High myopia beyond -8 dioptres more often calls for an ICL implant than a standard LASIK, with a medical decision that needs to be reviewed over time. At-risk corneas (asymmetric topography, borderline thickness, suspicion of subclinical keratoconus) require a thorough exam and often a decision to step away from corneal laser in favour of an alternative. Vision-critical professions (pilots, professional drivers, surgeons) demand an uncompromising result and a well-documented follow-up.

Marked pre-existing dry eye, age above 45 with early presbyopia, patients who have already had eye surgery or who carry an associated condition (diabetes, autoimmune disease) also require a thorough consultation and accessible long-term follow-up. Finally, any patient who values European legal security, consultation in their own language and continuity of the medical record benefits from having surgery in Belgium, even at a higher price.

Frequently asked questions

What patients often ask

How many trips are needed if I go to Turkey?

A medically rigorous pathway requires two separate trips: one for the preoperative exam, another for the procedure. Many Turkish centres instead propose a single 3 to 4 day stay, with exam and surgery almost back-to-back. Postoperative controls (D+1, D+7, D+30, M+3, M+12) then each require a trip or a handover to a local ophthalmologist.

What happens if a complication appears after returning home?

A complication must be managed quickly by a local ophthalmologist, who takes over the follow-up without access to the full operative record or the laser parameters used. Care is not refused, but it is more difficult than if the whole pathway had been carried out in one place. Identifying a local ophthalmologist before departure makes things easier.

Do UK, Swiss or French insurers reimburse surgery done in Turkey?

Refractive surgery is generally not reimbursed by public insurers, whether the procedure is done at home or abroad. Some supplementary insurers offer a flat allowance, sometimes conditional on the surgeon being based in the European Union. If a complication is treated at home after surgery performed in Turkey, routine consultations are covered under the same conditions as any other eye condition.

Can one eye be operated in Turkey and the other in Belgium?

It is possible but strongly discouraged. Both eyes should ideally be operated with the same parameters, on the same devices, with the same calibrated laser and by the same surgeon. Splitting the procedure between two teams creates asymmetries that are hard to correct.
